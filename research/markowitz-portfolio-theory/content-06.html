The three-asset benchmark

The benchmark specifies annual simple-return moments directly. No price download, forecast estimation or backtest is involved. The assets are labels A, B and C, with a positive-definite covariance derived from their stated volatilities and correlations.

Tangency and an investor allocation

With risk-free return 2%, tangency weights are approximately 54.6802%, 26.6797% and 18.6402%. The risky fund's expected return is 8.5584%, volatility is 10.6752%, and prospective Sharpe ratio is 0.614361. If borrowing is prohibited but the investor may allocate only between this fund and cash, the scalar optimum is capped at 100% risky capital.

A concentration cap

With each weight bounded above by 60%, the smallest feasible mean is \(0.6(0.06)+0.4(0.10)=7.6\%\). The largest is \(0.6(0.14)+0.4(0.10)=12.4\%\). A target of 13% is infeasible under those caps even though it is feasible without them.

This calculation checks feasibility independently of the optimiser. It also distinguishes a concentration cap from a short-sale restriction. A solver should identify the reason a target fails, rather than returning a renormalised approximation with a different expected return.

Risk shares for equal capital

Equal initial weights do not give equal risk shares. The benchmark's most volatile asset contributes much more to portfolio variation than asset A. The sum of absolute volatility contributions equals the portfolio volatility; the sum of normalised shares equals one.

Every numerical comparison holds the model and units fixed. Recompute the same quantities in the laboratory, then vary one input at a time. This connects the analytical expressions to the observable changes in the frontier and weights.

27 / Numerical methods

Implementation and numerical verification

A reliable implementation separates moment validation, the optimisation problem and portfolio reporting. Validate dimensions, symmetry and finiteness; identify the return horizon; check covariance definiteness; then solve the stated constraints. Report mean, variance, weights and residuals from the same validated inputs.

An unrestricted reference implementation

The following NumPy example solves positive-definite, fully invested GMV or exact-target problems. It handles equal means explicitly and avoids constructing the covariance inverse.

import numpy as np

def minimum_variance(mu, cov, target=None):
    mu = np.asarray(mu, dtype=float)
    cov = np.asarray(cov, dtype=float)
    n = mu.size
    if mu.ndim != 1 or n == 0 or cov.shape != (n, n):
        raise ValueError("Inconsistent dimensions")
    if not np.all(np.isfinite(mu)) or not np.all(np.isfinite(cov)):
        raise ValueError("Nonfinite moments")
    if not np.allclose(cov, cov.T, atol=1e-12, rtol=1e-10):
        raise ValueError("Covariance must be symmetric")
    np.linalg.cholesky(cov)  # requires positive definiteness
    one = np.ones(n)
    u = np.linalg.solve(cov, one)
    A = one @ u
    if target is None:
        w = u / A
    else:
        if not np.isfinite(target):
            raise ValueError("Nonfinite target")
        if np.ptp(mu) < 1e-10:
            if abs(target - mu[0]) > 1e-10:
                raise ValueError("Equal means: target infeasible")
            w = u / A
        else:
            F = np.column_stack([one, mu])
            X = np.linalg.solve(cov, F)
            multipliers = np.linalg.solve(F.T @ X, [1, target])
            w = X @ multipliers
    variance = w @ cov @ w
    return {"weights": w, "mean": w @ mu,
            "variance": variance, "volatility": np.sqrt(variance)}

The tolerance is a numerical convention for this example, not a general rank-detection policy across every data scale. A nearly redundant return constraint requires appropriate conditioning checks and scaling. Exact-target residuals should be verified after the solve.

Quadratic programming with constraints

A standard solver accepts a quadratic objective and affine equality and inequality constraints. Check whether it defines the objective with a one-half factor. Supply covariance accordingly, and verify its final status, primal feasibility, stationarity, complementarity and relevant dual signs.

A success message does not explain whether tolerances are economically appropriate. A weight residual of \(10^{-6}\) may be negligible for an illustration and material for a tight exposure mandate. Recompute the objective and constraints from the returned weights instead of trusting rounded display values.

Meaningful verification cases

For independent equal-volatility assets, GMV should be equal weight. For diagonal unequal covariance, it should be inverse variance. In the benchmark, the target mean and net budget should agree with their constraints, and computed variance should match the analytical frontier formula.

Long-only target variances cannot be below unrestricted target variances under identical positive-definite inputs. Infeasible targets should fail explicitly. Equal means should collapse the frontier. Invalid correlation matrices should be rejected even if all three pairwise entries lie within their permitted range.

Compare constrained results against an independent solver or a dense feasible grid for a small example. A grid supplies an upper bound on the attainable minimum, rather than an exact proof of optimality. For a convex quadratic, valid KKT conditions give a stronger certificate.

Statistical and implementation validation

Numerical tests establish that the code solves the supplied model. Statistical evaluation investigates whether those inputs and decisions generalise. Keep both: a correct solver cannot eliminate estimation error, and a profitable-looking historical result cannot establish solver correctness.

This makes the calculations reproducible without external libraries while preserving an explicit distinction between a teaching implementation and a large production optimisation system.

28 / Derivations

Mathematical appendix

Positive semidefinite covariance

For any fixed vector \(x\), expand the variance of its linear combination of returns:

\[ x^\mathsf{T}\Sigma x=\mathbb{E}\left[(x^\mathsf{T}(R-\mu))^2\right]\ge0. \]

This proves the necessary matrix property without an assumption of normality. It also shows why a covariance calculated from a common finite sample is positive semidefinite: its quadratic form is an average of squared projected sample deviations.

The frontier determinant

Use the inner product \(\langle a,b\rangle=a^\mathsf{T}\Sigma^{-1}b\). Cauchy–Schwarz gives

\[ (\mathbf{1}^\mathsf{T}\Sigma^{-1}\mu)^2\le(\mathbf{1}^\mathsf{T}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{1})(\mu^\mathsf{T}\Sigma^{-1}\mu). \]

Therefore \(D\ge0\). Equality requires linear dependence of the budget and mean vectors in this positive-definite metric, which means \(\mu=m_0\mathbf{1}\). Distinct means yield the strictly positive determinant needed by the analytical target formulas.

Budget-neutral efficient directions

Set \(d=\Sigma^{-1}(\mu-\mu_{\mathrm{GMV}}\mathbf{1})\). Its budget sum is \(B-A\mu_{\mathrm{GMV}}=0\), and its mean and variance satisfy

\[ \mu^\mathsf{T}d=\frac D A,\qquad d^\mathsf{T}\Sigma d=\frac D A. \]

Scaling \(d\) by \(A/D\) gives the direction \(h\) with unit mean change and variance \(A/D\). Orthogonality to GMV then yields \(\sigma^2(m)=1/A+(A/D)(m-\mu_{\mathrm{GMV}})^2\).

The tangency bound

Let \(a=\Sigma^{1/2}w\) and \(b=\Sigma^{-1/2}e\). Their inner product is \(w^\mathsf{T}e\). Cauchy–Schwarz therefore gives

\[ (w^\mathsf{T}e)^2\le(w^\mathsf{T}\Sigma w)(e^\mathsf{T}\Sigma^{-1}e). \]

Equality occurs for a covariance-adjusted excess-return direction. The squared inequality alone does not select its sign. Positive excess return and a positive normalising budget are the conditions behind the conventional fully invested tangency fund.

Uncertainty-set support

For \(\mu=\widehat\mu+U^{1/2}u\), minimise \(u^\mathsf{T}U^{1/2}w\) over \(\|u\|_2\le\kappa\). The minimizing direction opposes \(U^{1/2}w\), giving the penalty \(-\kappa\sqrt{w^\mathsf{T}Uw}\). If that vector is zero, the uncertain component has no effect on this portfolio.

Returns of an asset expressed in another currency

If \(R_{\mathrm{local}}\) is the local-currency return and \(R_{\mathrm{FX}}\) is the domestic-currency gain on one unit of that currency,

\[ R_{\mathrm{domestic}}=R_{\mathrm{local}}+R_{\mathrm{FX}}+R_{\mathrm{local}}R_{\mathrm{FX}}. \]

The interaction term matters for exact simple-return moments. A first-order additive approximation drops it. Currency hedging changes the payoff and financing structure, so covariance should be estimated for the actual hedged or unhedged return convention.

29 / Self-assessment

Exercises and solutions

Try each question before opening its solution. State units and constraints before calculating. Several exercises distinguish a mathematically valid allocation from the different problem an implementation may actually solve.

1. A weighted expected return

Assets have means 6%, 10% and 14%. What is the mean of a 50% / 30% / 20% portfolio? Does dependence enter this calculation?

Solution

The mean is 8.8%. Linearity of expectation holds regardless of dependence for fixed initial weights. Dependence enters variance and the distribution of outcomes, rather than this weighted mean identity.

2. A diversification calculation

Invest equally in assets with volatilities 10% and 20% and correlation zero. Calculate variance and volatility. Compare with the weighted average of volatilities.

Solution

Variance is \(0.25(0.10^2)+0.25(0.20^2)=0.0125\). Volatility is \(\sqrt{0.0125}\), approximately 11.1803%, below the 15% weighted average. With correlation one and positive weights, volatility would equal that 15% average.

3. An impossible correlation matrix

Three pairwise correlations are 0.9, 0.9 and −0.9. Each lies in \([-1,1]\). Can they jointly describe a correlation matrix?

Solution

No. The determinant is \(1+2(0.9)(0.9)(-0.9)-3(0.9^2)=-2.888\), which is negative. Pairwise plausibility does not ensure positive semidefiniteness.

4. Independent-asset GMV

For independent assets with volatilities 10% and 20%, derive the fully invested GMV weights. Are they inverse-volatility weights?

Solution

Inverse variances are 100 and 25, so GMV weights are 80% and 20%. Inverse-volatility weights would be two-thirds and one-third, which solve a different allocation rule. With equal uncorrelated variances, GMV becomes equal weight.

5. A dominated exact target

The benchmark GMV mean is approximately 6.9937%. An unrestricted exact-target problem is solved for 5%. Is its solution efficient?

Solution

It minimises variance at 5%, but lies on the lower branch. The symmetric upper-branch mean is approximately \(2(6.9937\%)-5\%=8.9875\%\), at the same minimum volatility. The upper point dominates it. A requirement of at least 5% would instead choose GMV.

6. A target outside the long-only range

Can a fully invested long-only benchmark portfolio have expected return 15%? What if short sales are allowed?

Solution

Long-only means lie between 6% and 14%, so 15% is infeasible. With unrestricted shorts and distinct means, the target is algebraically attainable but can require gross exposure above one. Financing or leverage constraints may still rule it out.

7. Net and gross exposures

Weights are 120%, 30% and −50%. Calculate net and gross exposure. What does a gross limit of 150% imply?

Solution

Net exposure is 100%, while gross exposure is 200%. The portfolio satisfies the net budget but violates a 150% gross limit. Removing the short and renormalising would create a different portfolio, rather than solve the original constrained problem.

8. Scaling investor risk aversion

Under unrestricted cash-inclusive mean–variance utility, what happens to risky holdings when \(\gamma\) doubles and all moments stay fixed?

Solution

The risky vector \(x^\star=\Sigma^{-1}e/\gamma\) halves. The risky fund's composition remains unchanged when the conventional tangency exists; the cash fraction changes to keep total initial wealth at one. This conclusion can fail when borrowing or asset constraints bind.

9. Equal capital and risk

For two independent assets with volatilities 10% and 20%, what capital weights give equal risk contributions? Compare with the GMV allocation.

Solution

Equality of variance contributions requires \(w_1^2(0.10^2)=w_2^2(0.20^2)\). For positive weights, \(w_1=2w_2\), giving two-thirds and one-third. GMV is 80% / 20%. Equal risk and minimum variance are different objectives.

10. An optimistic validation design

A researcher tries twenty shrinkage intensities, selects the one with lowest risk in a future test period, then reports that period as an untouched performance evaluation. What is wrong?

Solution

The test period was used to choose the intensity and has become validation data. Its reported minimum benefits from selection on noise. Use a separate final evaluation period or an appropriately nested time-series design, and document the choices examined.
