01 / Research question

When does a winning bet become a losing strategy?

The project asks how the fraction of capital staked on a favourable, repeated bet changes long-run growth and the risk of deep losses, and whether simulation reproduces the analytical answer given by the Kelly criterion.

What I built

A closed-form model of expected log-growth; an in-browser simulator with a one-bet-per-click mode and a seeded Monte Carlo mode (up to 10,000 paths of 1,000 bets); an experiment in which the bettor overestimates the edge; and an independent NumPy implementation, with pytest checks, that reproduces the page's precomputed figures from the same seed.

What the evidence establishes

Inside this model, the simulated mean log-growth matches the analytical value, growth peaks at the Kelly fraction and turns negative near twice that fraction, and the browser and Python produce the same seeded results. This establishes numerical self-consistency of a stylised betting model, not a profitable strategy or the behaviour of real markets.

The baseline uses a win probability of 0.55, even-money odds, 200 bets per path, 5,000 paths and seed 42. Capital is normalised to 1, so wealth is reported as a multiple of the starting stake: 2.72× means 2.72 times the initial capital.

02 / Mathematical framework

One bet, repeated: the model

Each round the bettor stakes a constant fraction of current wealth. With probability p the bet wins and pays b per unit staked (net odds of b to 1); with probability q = 1 − p the stake is lost. Outcomes are independent across rounds.

Wt+1 = Wt (1 + b·f)   with probability p
Wt+1 = Wt (1 − f)     with probability q = 1 − p

Because wealth compounds multiplicatively, the quantity that governs the long run is the expected logarithm of the growth factor, not expected wealth. By the law of large numbers, the average log-return per bet converges to:

1N ln WNW0  →  g(f) = p·ln(1 + b·f) + (1 − p)·ln(1 − f)

Setting the first derivative to zero gives the growth-optimal stake, the Kelly fraction:

g′(f) = p·b1 + b·f − q1 − f = 0   ⇒   f* = b·p − qb

No edge, no bet. If the edge b·p − q is zero or negative, g decreases for every positive stake and the optimum is f* = 0. The simulator then flags that there is no positive edge, and the Kelly path stays flat.

Why overbetting turns growth negative

The function g is strictly concave: it is zero at f = 0, rises to its maximum at f*, then falls and crosses zero again at a fraction f₀. For small edges, a second-order expansion makes the symmetry visible:

g(f) ≈ (b·p − q)·f − 12(b²p + q)·f²
f₀ ≈ 2(b·p − q)b²p + q   (≈ 2f* when b = 1)

With even money, b²p + q = 1, so the quadratic approximation returns f* exactly and places f₀ at about 2f*. In the baseline, f* = 0.100 and the exact root, found by bisection, is f₀ = 0.1987. Staking 20% of wealth on a 55% coin flip therefore has a slightly negative expected log-growth, even though every single bet has positive expected value.

Expected wealth tells a different story: E[WN] = W0(1 + f(b·p − q))N grows for any positive stake when the edge is positive. The mean is carried by a few very lucky paths; the typical, median path follows approximately W0·exp(N·g(f)). That gap is the whole problem.

04 / Model risk

When your edge is smaller than you think

Kelly needs the true probability. A bettor who believes p = 0.60 when the true value is 0.53 computes a stake of 0.20 instead of 0.06: more than three times the growth-optimal fraction. This experiment generates outcomes with the true p and compares three rules on the same outcomes. It reuses the odds, number of bets, number of paths and seed from the simulator above.

RuleStake fg(f) at true probabilityMedian final capitalP(capital < 10%)P(drawdown > 50%)

Why practitioners use fractional Kelly. Overestimating p pushes the full-Kelly stake past the true root f₀, where expected log-growth is negative. Half Kelly gives up about a quarter of the growth rate (in the quadratic approximation, g(f*/2) ≈ ¾·g(f*)), but with even money it can absorb an edge overestimated by a factor of up to about four before growth turns negative, against a factor of about two for full Kelly.

Additional background
In a continuous-time approximation, the probability that wealth ever falls to a fraction x of its initial value is x for full Kelly and x2/c − 1 for a constant fraction c of Kelly (Thorp, 2006). At x = 0.1 that is 10% for full Kelly and 0.1% for half Kelly. These are infinite-horizon approximations; the tables on this page report simulated frequencies over a finite number of discrete bets.

The simulated mean log-growth sits within two standard errors of g(f) in every row. That is what the law of large numbers predicts. It is not an independent confirmation of the formula, because both quantities come from the same model.

Automated tests

  • Maximum of g. A dense grid search and a golden-section search locate the maximum of g at the analytical f* for six combinations of p and b, and the first-order condition is zero at f*.
  • Convergence. For four combinations of p, b and f, the simulated mean log-growth lies within four standard errors of g(f) at three sample sizes, and the standard error shrinks as one over the square root of the number of bets.
  • Limiting cases. A fair even-money bet (p = 0.5, b = 1) gives f* = 0 and negative growth for any stake; negative edges give f* = 0; a zero stake keeps capital exactly constant on every path.
  • Generator and page. The vectorised NumPy generator matches a literal transcription of the JavaScript generator, the root f₀ is a zero of g, and the static figures embedded in this page match a fresh run of the Python code.

06 / What the results can and cannot say

Reliability, assumptions and limits

The simulation validates the mathematics of a stylised bet under its own assumptions. Its precision measures Monte Carlo noise conditional on those assumptions; it says nothing about how far real bets or markets are from them.

AssumptionWhat it enables hereWhere reality differs
Independent, identically distributed betsThe law of large numbers, and g(f) as the long-run growth rateReal returns show dependence, changing regimes and fat tails.
p and b known exactlyf* computed without errorp must be estimated; section 04 shows the cost of overestimating it.
No costs, taxes or spreadsGrowth factors of exactly 1 + b·f or 1 − fFrictions lower the effective odds and the optimal stake.
No liquidity limits or maximum stakeStakes scale with wealth indefinitelyMarkets and bookmakers cap size, and large positions move prices.
No minimum bet; stakes infinitely divisibleWealth never reaches exactly zeroMinimum sizes create genuine ruin at low wealth, hence the 10% convention.
One binary bet per roundA closed-form f*Several simultaneous or correlated positions need a multivariate version.

What it shows

For a known, repeated edge, the stake decides the typical outcome; growth is maximised at f*; staking beyond about 2f* makes the typical path shrink; and full Kelly still carries deep drawdowns.

What it does not show

That any real bet or asset has a known edge, that Kelly sizing is suitable for a particular person, or that the simulated frequencies apply to markets. Kelly ignores risk preferences other than log utility.

Simulation noise

Probabilities from 5,000 paths have a standard error of at most about 0.7 percentage points. The generator, mulberry32, is fast and adequate for visualisation, but it is not a research-grade or cryptographic generator.

07 / Reproducibility

How to inspect and rerun the work

The page is a single HTML file with no external libraries, fonts or network requests; open it directly in a browser. The Python files are independent of it: kelly_sim.py reimplements the model and the generator in NumPy and writes the figures of section 05, and test_kelly.py checks both the mathematics and the figures embedded in the page.

08 / Conclusion

The edge sets the ceiling; the stake decides the outcome

With the same 55% even-money bet and the same outcomes, staking 10% of wealth took the median path to 2.72× in 200 bets, staking 20% left it at 0.97×, and staking 30% took it to 0.04×, with 80% of paths falling below a tenth of their capital.

Kelly maximises long-run growth, but it is aggressive: 93% of baseline paths staking f* suffered a drawdown of more than 50% within 200 bets. When the edge itself is uncertain, overestimating it is far more costly than underestimating it, which is why fractional Kelly is the defensible default. In the misestimated scenario, full Kelly on the believed edge fell below 10% of its capital in 60% of paths; half Kelly did so in 7%.

Natural extensions would estimate p with explicit uncertainty, add transaction costs and minimum stakes, and size several correlated bets at once. Those require new assumptions and are not claimed by this project.
